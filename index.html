The Coastal Paradox

How the Pacific Islands' greatest asset can also be its greatest vulnerability

A region measured in water, not land

Despite their limited land areas, Pacific Islands are surrounded by vast expanses of ocean. This geography provides significant economic and strategic value, while also concentrating communities, infrastructure, and economic activity along exposed coastlines.

Twenty-two countries and territories

The Pacific's low-lying nations are built around water. Their economies depend on the ocean for food and trade, and international law grants them jurisdiction over some of the largest maritime territories on earth. Twenty-two countries and territories are spread across this region, and on a standard map, most appear as small marks against a vast expanse of ocean, their combined land area a fraction of the region they occupy.

Small islands, vast borders

Adding each country's exclusive economic zone changes the proportions considerably. Nations with minimal land area hold jurisdiction over ocean territory extending thousands of kilometres from their shores. For example, Kiribati governs a maritime area roughly the size of India, despite a total land area close to that of New York City. With little to no inland territory, most of these nations lack a safe interior. Housing, infrastructure, and economies are pressed against the shoreline, leaving nowhere else to build.

The risks are not occasional

These risks are persistent and have tangible consequences for communities and economies across the region. Disasters recur across the Pacific year after year, taking different forms and producing consequences that extend from individual communities to national economies.

A recurring pattern of disaster

The chart shows the number of disasters reported in Pacific Islands each year over two decades. The figure fluctuates considerably, with 2015 witnessing the highest number of disastrous events. This region has not experienced a disaster-free year since this record began.

A spectrum of hazards

Exploring disasters by hazard type reveals the different forces shaping the region's disaster profile. Meteorological events, including cyclones and severe storms, account for the largest share of disasters in most years. Hydrological hazards, such as floods, and climatological hazards, including prolonged droughts, accounts for much of the remaining share and have become increasingly prominent over time. The region is also exposed to geophysical hazards such as earthquakes and tsunamis, alongside biological hazards such as disease outbreaks and pest infestations.

Measuring impact on human

The true scale of a disaster is best understood by not only how often it strikes but also how many people it affects. Grouping this data into units of 10,000 individuals makes the escalating number of people affected by disasters clearer. The baseline of human impact has risen steadily over the past decade, creating a sharp contrast with the quieter early 2000s. This shift is largely driven by dense clusters of meteorological and geophysical disasters, along with a massive surge in the number of people affected by climatological disasters in 2015.

The economic burden

Disasters not only disrupt lives but also drain economies. Transitioning from human impact to financial cost, this chart presents the average annual loss in few countries of Pacific Islands. Fiji has experienced the largest economic burden, with a cost of approximately 88 millions USD on average. While most other nations face average annual losses under 50 millions of US dollars on average, this massive gap is driven by a combination of Fiji's frequent exposure to the hazards shown above and the dense, high-value infrastructure at risk within a larger economy.

Behind the rising risks

The growing consequences of these disasters cannot be separated from a changing physical environment. Warmer seas, rising sea levels, and increasingly variable rainfall are reshaping the conditions in which Pacific communities live and prepare for future hazards.

The sea is warmer ...

Records of sea surface temperature anomalies since 1850 show a distinct break from the past. For most of this period, annual values remained close to the long-term average, fluctuating within a relatively narrow band. In recent decades, however, temperatures have surged well above this historical range. This excess heat is critical, as warmer surface water increases the energy available to tropical cyclones, driving their formation and intensification.

... and higher ...

Sea level in the western Pacific has risen faster than the global average over the same period. As a result, coastal flooding and storm surge now originate from a higher baseline than in previous decades. While an annual increase measured in millimetres might sound insignificant, the cumulative effect is severe: a storm of comparable strength today produces greater coastal impact than an equivalent storm several decades ago.

... and more erratic

Rainfall patterns do not show a simple long-term increase or decrease in precipitation. Instead, they reveal growing variability over time. Earlier decades saw rainfall anomalies remain within a relatively narrow range, while recent years have experienced increasingly pronounced fluctuations. Severe deficits have been followed by substantial surpluses, including an unprecedented positive spike in 2019. This increasing variability poses significant challenges for communities that depend on predictable rainfall for drinking water and agriculture. Preparing for a generally drier or wetter climate is possible, but adapting to increasingly pronounced extremes in both directions presents a much greater challenge.

A disproportionate burden

The changing climate is driven by human activity, but its consequences are not distributed evenly. This chart illustrates greenhouse gas emissions per capita across the Pacific over the past fifty years on a logarithmic scale. Most Pacific nations have maintained very low levels of emissions per person throughout the period. Palau and New Caledonia are notable exceptions, with per-capita emissions substantially higher than elsewhere in the region. In New Caledonia, emissions are largely associated with its coal- and oil-powered nickel mining and processing industry, while Palau's higher per-capita figure reflects its reliance on diesel for electricity generation and fuel-intensive transport and tourism. Beyond these two outliers, the countries heavily affected by the environmental consequences of the changing climate have contributed relatively little to the greenhouse gas emissions.

Local adaptation and policy

Facing the risks of disaster every year, local governments across Pacific Islands have to take direct action. The following section examines the internal policy measures and adaptation strategies these nations have enacted on the ground.

Environmental taxes as a fiscal tool

Fiscal policy assists governments to address environmental risks and strengthen resilience, one of which is environmental taxation. This chart compares environmental tax revenue as a share of GDP across selected Pacific nations in 2020. Solomon Islands stands out with revenue equivalent to 3.80% of GDP, followed by Fiji, Nauru, and Papua New Guinea, each recording less than 0.5% of GDP. Environmental taxes on fuel, vehicles, waste, and resource extraction provide governments with a fiscal instrument to address environmental risks and strengthen resilience, yet their limited use across the region suggests that this policy tool remains largely underutilised.

The shift to renewable energy

Beyond fiscal measures, energy policy is also important for strengthening resilience. Preparing for disasters also means securing a reliable energy supply that supports essential economic and social activities. This chart illustrates the share of renewable energy in total final energy consumption for each country in the region from 2000 to the present. Several countries show a steady increase in the share of renewables over two decades, while others remain close to their starting levels. Papua New Guinea and the Solomon Islands report the highest renewable shares in the region, while countries such as Fiji, Kiribati, and Tuvalu remain below 10%. These lower shares reflect continued reliance on imported fuels, particularly diesel. Expanding renewable energy can reduce this dependence and strengthen energy security when disasters disrupt fuel supply.

Disaster planning at the local level

Effective disaster preparation depends not only on national policy but also on the implementation by local governments. The charts show the proportion of local governments that had adopted and were implementing a disaster risk reduction strategy as of 2021. Each grid contains 100 squares, with each filled square representing 1 percentage point. Palau, Vanuatu, Tuvalu, and Nauru report full coverage, while other countries show substantially lower levels of implementation, with several reporting no local governments with an active strategy. These differences highlight how unevenly national disaster preparedness has been translated into action at the local level, where evacuations and immediate disaster responses are coordinated.

The Challenge Ahead

From environmental change to economic and social pressures, challenges across the Pacific are persistent, interconnected, and far-reaching. The same waters that provide food, trade, and vast maritime territories also expose its twenty-two countries and territories to persistent disasters, rising sea levels, warmer temperatures, and increasingly erratic rainfall. These risks carry significant human and economic costs to many Pacific nations, despite their minor contributions to the greenhouse gas emissions driving global climate change. Governments are responding through disaster risk reduction planning, renewable energy adoption, and environmental taxation, but the extent and capacity of these measures vary considerably across the region. Taken together, the evidence points to a region that is highly exposed, unequally equipped to respond, and largely affected by forces beyond its control. The ocean remains both the Pacific's greatest asset and its greatest vulnerability.
